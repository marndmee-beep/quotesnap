Getting paid on time

Most late payments are an admin problem, not a bad client. Fix the wording up front and the chasing mostly disappears.

Put the terms on the quote, not just the contract

The quote is what the customer reads carefully. Whatever you agree there is what they remember, so state the deposit and the payment window on the quote itself.

Wording you can copy

Deposit: 30% payable to book the work. The balance is due on completion, within 7 days of invoice.
Payment due within 14 days of invoice date. Late payments may incur interest at 2% per month.
This quote is valid for 14 days. Prices held for that period.
The deposit is non-refundable, as it reserves time in the schedule that cannot be sold to another customer.

Check what your local law allows before charging late-payment interest, and have a professional review anything you rely on for a large job.

A reminder sequence that stays friendly

  • Due date minus two days: "Just a note that invoice 014 is due Thursday." No pressure, no apology.
  • Two days overdue: "Invoice 014 was due Tuesday — could you confirm when it will be paid?"
  • Two weeks overdue: a phone call, not an email. Ask whether there is a problem with the invoice itself.
  • Four weeks overdue: pause new work and say so plainly, in writing.

Deposits

A deposit does two jobs: it gets you paid for time you have blocked out, and it filters out people who were never going to book. For work where you buy materials up front, ask for enough to cover them.

If a client goes quiet

  • Check the invoice reached the right person. A surprising share of unpaid invoices were sent to an address nobody monitors.
  • Ask a specific question with a date in it, rather than "any update?".
  • Keep every message in writing and dated. If it ever escalates, a clean trail does most of the work.
