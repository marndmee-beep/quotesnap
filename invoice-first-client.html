How to invoice a client for the first time

Your first invoice is mostly about being clear and being on time. Here's the whole thing.

What has to be on it

  • An invoice number. Start at 001 and count up. Never reuse a number.
  • The date you issue it, and the date payment is due.
  • Your details: business name, address, email, and any tax registration number you hold.
  • Your client's details: the legal name of the business and its billing address.
  • What you did, as a line per item, with quantity and price.
  • The total, plus tax if you charge it.
  • How to pay you: bank details or a payment link.

Numbering and records

Keep a simple log of invoice number, client, amount and date sent. If a client loses your invoice, resending it is a single line from the log rather than a reconstruction from memory. Tax authorities generally expect you to keep these records for several years, so a spreadsheet or folder that survives is enough at this size.

When to send it

Send it the day the work is done, or on the date you agreed in the contract. Monthly retainers bill on a fixed date each month. The single biggest reason freelancers get paid late is invoicing late.

Payment terms that work

"Payment due within 14 days" is a reasonable default for small businesses. Larger clients often run 30 or 45 days, and there is nothing wrong with that if you agree it up front rather than discovering it after the work is finished. For new clients, a deposit of 30 to 50 percent before you start removes most of the risk.

Common first-invoice mistakes

  • Billing the wrong legal entity, a trading name rather than the company that will pay.
  • Forgetting to say what the price excludes, such as travel or third-party costs.
  • Sending it as a screenshot or in the body of an email. Send a PDF, and name the file with the invoice number.
